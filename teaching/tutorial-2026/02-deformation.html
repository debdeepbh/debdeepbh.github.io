Fracture and sustainability

  • Understanding of fracture mechanism can lead to accurate simulations: economic sustainability
  • Understanding how structures fail under loading conditions can lead to sustainable design practices: reducing waste
  • Reinforcement near failure-prone zones in the domain can increase durability, e.g. prosthetics, wind turbine blades

Fracture mechanism in sea-ice modeling

  • Breakage in sea-ice floes lead to large-scale sea-ice concentration change (seasonal vs multi-year)
  • Fracture and melting are coupled, impacting earth’s climate system
  • Connectivity of granular ice is important for arctic ecology (i.e. polar bear). Fracture disrupts food supply.

Science of Material Failure

  • The word failure was first used in the sense of breakdown in 1793 (Smeaton), English engineer, to describe a breaking of a bolt in a lighthouse he built

  • Fracture is associated with bones, which is undesired

  • Controlled fracture is desired in some cases: stone construction, cutting, tearing, polishing

  • Industrial revolution introduced fracture problems that need to be solved, mainly due to corrosion and wear

Deformation of materials

  • Hooke’s law is an empirical law which states that the force (F) needed to extend or compress (or expand) a spring by some distance (x) scales linearly with respect to that distance.

\[F = kx\] where k is a constant factor characteristic of the spring (i.e., its stiffness), and x is small compared to the total possible deformation of the spring.

as the extension so the force

– Robert Hooke (1635-1703)

Quantities

  • Spring constant \(k\)

  • Strain: rate of change of deformation with respect to length

  • Stress: force per unit area

2D

  • Normal strain \(\epsilon\)
  • Shear strain \(\gamma\)
  • Lame parameters
    • Young’s modulus \(E = \sigma/\epsilon\)
    • Shear modulus \(\mu = \tau/\gamma\)
  • Poisson ratio \(\nu\): the deformation (expansion or contraction) of a material in directions perpendicular to the specific direction of loading.
  • Only two parameters are enough to describe a material: \(\mu = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}\)
